# E-E-A-T and author trust

Read this file with a clear boundary in mind. E-E-A-T is a Google
Search quality framework. It is NOT an AdSense approval rule, and
Google does not publish an AdSense checklist that mentions E-E-A-T.
This file explains the framework accurately, then maps the trust
signals to review readiness as INFERRED practice guidance. The mapping
is a judgement call, not a confirmed Google rule.

## What E-E-A-T is

Source: Google Search Central, "Creating helpful, reliable,
people-first content"
(`developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content`),
read live on 18 November 2026. Status: OFFICIAL.

E-E-A-T stands for Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and
Trustworthiness. The page defines them as follows:

- **Experience:** the content creator has first-hand, real experience
  with the topic. A product review by someone who used the product, or
  a guide by someone who visited the place, carries experience.
- **Expertise:** the creator has the knowledge or skill the topic
  needs. A tax guide written by a qualified accountant carries
  expertise.
- **Authoritativeness:** the creator or site is a recognised go-to
  source for the topic.
- **Trustworthiness:** the site and content are accurate, honest, and
  safe. The page calls trust the most important member of the family,
  and says the other members support it. A page with low trust scores
  low on E-E-A-T even if it looks experienced, expert, and
  authoritative.

The same page states plainly that E-E-A-T itself is not a specific
ranking factor. It is part of a mix of factors that shape how
Google's systems judge whether content is helpful and reliable, and it
is used by human quality raters whose feedback improves those
systems.

## Where E-E-A-T weighs more

The page gives extra weight to E-E-A-T for topics that can affect
health, money, safety, or wellbeing, often called YMYL topics, short
for "your money or your life". A medical explainer, a loan guide, or a
legal how-to is judged more strictly than a hobby post. For those
topics, the page expects clear evidence of who wrote the content and
why they are qualified to write it.

## The Who, How, and Why questions

The page asks publishers to answer three questions about every piece
of content:

- **Who:** who created the content. Bylines and author information
  help readers judge trust. A page with no author information at all
  gives the reader nothing to judge.
- **How:** how the content was produced. For AI-assisted content, the
  page says disclosure is useful where readers would reasonably expect
  it. See `41-ai-assisted-and-scaled-content.md`.
- **Why:** why the content was created. Content made mainly to help
  people is judged differently from content made mainly to manipulate
  rankings or chase ad revenue.

## Deceptive authorship is a low-quality signal

The page warns against presenting content as if it came from a person
or organisation it did not come from. Examples named on the page
include a made-up author name, a fake author photo, false credentials,
and claiming first-hand experience that never happened. This applies
to every site in this skill's scope: never invent an author, never use
a stock or AI-generated face as if it were the author's photo, and
never claim a test, visit, or result that did not happen. Real names,
real photos the author consents to share, and honest experience claims
are the only safe approach.

## Main content quality

The page lists what counts toward the quality of the main content of a
page: effort, originality, talent or skill, and accuracy. Effort alone
is not enough. A long page that repeats what every other page says,
with no added value, is still low quality. A short page that fully
answers its question can be high quality. The page also answers the
word-count question directly: Google has no preferred word count, and
no minimum length is published.

## Mapping to AdSense review readiness

Status: INFERRED. The mapping below is practice guidance built from
the framework above and from the rejection patterns in
`07-rejection-reasons-and-fixes.md`. Google does not confirm that its
site reviewers score E-E-A-T. Treat every item as a trust signal that
helps a human reviewer and a reader, not as a pass condition.

- **Author pages:** every author has a dedicated page with a short
  bio, a real photo or a clearly labelled monogram, and links to the
  posts they wrote. The bio states what the author actually does and
  what experience they have with the topic.
- **Bios on posts:** each post shows the author name and a short bio
  or a link to the author page. The name on the post matches the name
  on the author page.
- **Person schema:** structured data using the Person type can state
  the author's name, job title, and same-as links in a machine-readable
  form. Schema is a Search feature, not an AdSense requirement, and it
  never substitutes for visible author information on the page.
- **About page:** the site explains who runs it, why it exists, and
  how content is produced. The About story must be true. If the site is
  a one-person project, say so.
- **Contact page:** a working contact route, with a real email or form
  that is monitored. A contact page that goes nowhere hurts trust.
- **Sources on YMYL claims:** health, finance, and legal figures carry
  a named source with a date. Unsourced numbers on a YMYL page are a
  trust risk.
- **Consistent identity:** the author name, site name, and contact
  details are the same across the site, the About page, and the
  account application. Mismatches raise questions.

## What E-E-A-T does not do

- It does not guarantee AdSense approval. A site with perfect author
  pages can still be rejected for low value content if the posts
  themselves are thin.
- It does not replace content quality. Author signals support good
  content. They do not rescue bad content.
- It is not a score. There is no E-E-A-T score in Search Console or
  AdSense, and no tool can measure it. Any tool that claims an E-E-A-T
  score is guessing.
