# Publisher scams and protection

AdSense approval is valuable, and that value attracts scams. The
patterns below are the ones publishers report most often. The policy
point about account transfers is labelled with its source. Everything
else in this file is practical guidance, not Google policy. It is
written to help a publisher recognise a scam before money or account
access changes hands.

## Buying or selling an approved account

Status: REPORTED. The AdSense Help page "Can I transfer my AdSense
account to someone else?" (answer 160202) states in its official
search snippet, read on 18 November 2026, that the AdSense Terms and
Conditions do not permit transfers of account ownership. The AdSense
Terms and Conditions assignment clause, as filed with the US
Securities and Exchange Commission, likewise restricts assignment.
The direct Help page fetch failed with a rate-limit error on 18
November 2026, so this point is labelled REPORTED rather than OFFICIAL
and the live page should be rechecked before quoting it.

What this means in practice:

- An "approved AdSense account for sale" is an account whose ownership
  cannot legally move to you under the Terms. The seller keeps the
  underlying Google Account, the payee identity, and the recovery
  options.
- The buyer can lose the account, the balance, and the money paid, all
  at once, when the seller recovers the account or Google detects the
  transfer.
- A bought account also carries the seller's history: prior policy
  flags, invalid traffic signals, and payment holds the buyer cannot
  see.
- Do not buy, sell, rent, or "share" an AdSense account. Apply with
  your own Google Account, your own identity, and your own site.

## Fake approval services

The common pitch is a guaranteed approval in a fixed number of days,
for a fee, often with screenshots of other people's dashboards as
proof. The red flags:

- A guarantee. Nobody outside Google can guarantee approval. The
  review decision is Google's, and no service has a back door.
- A claim of insider access. "My contact in the review team" is a
  standard scam line. Google's review teams do not sell approvals.
- A request for your Google Account password, recovery codes, or
  two-factor codes. No legitimate service needs these. Handing them
  over hands over the account.
- A request to add the service as a user on your account with full
  access, or to change the payee details to theirs.
- Payment by irreversible methods only, with no written contract and
  no refund route.
- A promise to "fix" a rejection by opening a second account, changing
  the site's ownership details, or hiding content from reviewers. Each
  of those is itself a policy risk.

What to do instead: fix the site yourself using
`07-rejection-reasons-and-fixes.md` and `08-pre-apply-checklist.md`,
or hire help for clearly defined work (writing, design, technical
fixes) where you keep full control of the account and the site. Pay
for work, never for a promised outcome.

## Fake support numbers and phishing

Status: practical guidance. The research for this skill found no
verified official AdSense phone number published by Google, as of 18
November 2026. AdSense support runs through the Help Center, the
account's contact options, and the Policy Center. Treat any phone
number that claims to be AdSense support as unverified until proven
otherwise through an official Google page.

Phishing patterns to recognise:

- An email or message claiming your account will be closed today
  unless you click a link and sign in. Real Google notices appear
  inside the account as well. Check the account directly, never
  through the link in the message.
- A sender address that looks close to Google but is not on a google.com
  domain. Display names are easy to fake. Read the actual address.
- A page that looks like a Google sign-in page but sits on a different
  domain. Check the address bar before entering anything.
- A request for an OTP, a backup code, or a password reset link.
  Google support never asks for these in a chat or email.
- A message offering to "release" a held payment for a fee, or asking
  for bank details by email or chat. Payment issues are handled inside
  the account and through official support channels only.
- A social media account with a Google logo offering help for a fee.
  Official Google support accounts do not charge for help and do not
  handle account issues over social media direct messages.

If you clicked a phishing link or shared credentials, change the
Google Account password immediately, review the account's security
activity, revoke unfamiliar sessions and app passwords, and turn on
two-factor authentication if it is not already on. Then check the
AdSense account for changed payee details, added users, or changed
payment methods.

## Unrealistic guarantees and income claims

- "Earn a fixed amount per day" claims are a red flag. AdSense
  earnings depend on traffic, geography, niche, ad demand, and
  seasonality. Nobody can promise a daily figure.
- Screenshots of earnings prove nothing. They can be edited, borrowed,
  or taken from a different account. Ask for the method, not the
  screenshot.
- Courses that promise approval if you follow a secret checklist are
  selling the checklist, not the approval. The real checklist is
  public: the Help Center, the Program policies, and this skill's
  references, all free.
- "Done for you" sites sold as approval-ready often contain copied or
  AI-generated content with no original value. The buyer inherits the
  rejection risk. Check the content yourself before buying any site.

## Protecting the account

Practical habits that cost nothing:

- Use a strong, unique password for the Google Account that owns the
  AdSense account, and turn on two-factor authentication.
- Never share the password, OTPs, or backup codes with anyone,
  including someone claiming to be Google support.
- Review the account's user access regularly. Remove anyone who no
  longer needs access.
- Check payee details and payment methods inside the account
  regularly. A changed bank account or payee name is the first sign of
  a compromised account.
- Keep the recovery email and phone number current, so a lockout can
  be recovered through official routes.
- Report phishing emails through the email provider's report function
  and, where the account offers it, through Google's phishing report
  route. Do not reply to the phisher.
